## Meeting Notes
- Hillhouse overview and strategy
  - Hillhouse is a global investment firm with roots in China and over 100 billion USD AUM, investing across private equity, venture, growth, real estate, private credit, and public markets.
  - ~90% of LP capital is from outside China (US, Middle East, Europe); LPs include endowments, foundations, pensions, and sovereign wealth funds.
  - Notable deals include a 4 billion euro carve-out of Philips’ home appliance business.
  - Emphasis on long-term partnerships and value creation via a 200-person portfolio development team covering finance, operations, talent, supply chain, and site build-outs.
  - Conclusion: Hillhouse positions itself as a long-term, value-adding partner with global capabilities and dedicated post-investment support.
- Portfolio development team introductions
  - Fiona (portfolio development) focuses on financial/operational support; based in Beijing; joined in 2018. Team includes Angela (reports to Fiona), Yao Yang (European/Siemens background; supports EU site build), and Céline (global talent hiring).
  - External operating advisor: Dr. Ralph Fuhrmann (c. 40 years in pharma/biotech/chemicals; CEO in Germany/Italy) to oversee Europe execution.
  - Conclusion: A blended in-house and advisor team will support European expansion.
- Nestlé context and participants’ backgrounds
  - Attendees highlighted multi-region procurement and M&A experience across Latin America, Europe, Asia Pacific, and the US.
  - Nestlé’s M&A model often keeps 80/20 control, with Nestlé chairing boards and founders retained as CEOs (e.g., Shanghai seasoning brand).
  - Overview of Nestlé: 160-year history; operations in 186 countries with 335 factories; businesses include Beverages, Pet Care, Nutrition & Health Science, Culinary, Frozen Foods (US), Milk & Ice Cream, Confectionery, and Water (premium retained; low-cost divested).
  - Strong R&D footprint (~26 centers, thousands of PhDs); internal AI tools (Nes GPT, Microsoft Copilot) with ChatGPT restricted.
  - Conclusion: Nestlé is a science- and brand-driven F&B leader with extensive M&A and global operations, familiar with balanced post-M&A models.
- Investment in Lingzyme (Lingzhen/Lingran; enzyme platform vision) and governance
  - Hillhouse USD fund invested end of last year; holds ~60%; founder family remains significant shareholder and in management.
  - Board: five members; three from Hillhouse entity (Piaflox) and two from Mingbang; Chair: Mr. Lin; CEO/General Manager: Mrs. Ye; legal representative: Chuan.
  - Conclusion: Governance aims to combine founder continuity with Hillhouse support.
- Technology differentiation of Lingzyme
  - Core strength: proprietary affinity chromatography resin enabling high-purity, high-activity enzyme purification (e.g., trypsin >4,000 activity).
  - Market resins (e.g., Cytiva/GE, Thermo Fisher) cost ~10,000 USD/L; Lingzyme’s in-house resin reduces costs for mass production.
  - Applicable to both animal-sourced and fermentation-derived enzymes.
  - Conclusion: Cost-effective, high-end affinity purification at scale is Lingzyme’s key advantage.
- Dual-grade production approach (pharma and food)
  - Discussion on running pharma-grade and food-grade trypsin on the same line; documentation will differ by application despite similar processes.
  - Digital process validation can cover both API and food applications.
  - EFSA dossier: two-step virus validation performed in China (data from a German lab); plan to include EU factory data in future submissions.
  - Conclusion: Leverage shared processes with differentiated documentation; support EFSA dossier with EU site validation data.
- European expansion plan and Nestlé collaboration
  - Objective: Build a European factory as first global step to better serve key clients (e.g., Nestlé); ensure full EU compliance including EU GMP; source EU pancreases; manufacture in Europe.
  - Raw materials: Partnership with Taurm/Terramont (Italy-based EU GMP pancreatin provider) for high-quality pancreas sourcing; Terramont to support land, supply chain security, and permit acceleration.
  - Quality and safety: Transfer China-proven technology to Europe; conduct European-standard virus clearance validation.
  - Market access: Expect support for registering products in Germany and qualifying Lingzyme as a supplier (leveraging Nestlé/MUCOS context).
  - Conclusion: Plan centers on compliant EU production, validated quality, and formal German market registration with partner support.
- Ownership, partnership, and JV structure for EU site
  - Linzyme plans absolute operational control, with on-site management and technology; intends to retain ~80–90% equity.
  - Terramont/Natics to receive a small minority stake to align interests; alternative to rent land from Terramont with JV specifics pending.
  - Counterpart indicated ~90% Linzyme and ~10% Natics/Terramont is acceptable; risk that Natics/Terramont “know too much” noted but not seen as a major issue.
  - Conclusion: Ownership structure trending toward Linzyme control with limited strategic minority participation; final structure to be confirmed.
- Relationship among Natics, Terramont, and Medix
  - Natics previously held ~20% of Terramont; Terramont produces EU-GMP pancreatin but faced EU-GMP issues for two years, halting production and causing liquidity problems.
  - Medix acquired 90% of Terramont last year and is now the majority shareholder.
  - Concern: if EU factories resolve supply, Natics’ invoicing/business could drop significantly.
  - Conclusion: Stakeholders recognize the Natics–Terramont–Medix links and potential impacts.
- European site scouting, cost learnings, and capacity plan
  - Extensive search (c. 30 site visits) across Germany and Eastern Europe; Germany option near Leipzig (Dow industrial park) paused after independent estimate (GICON) of ~€30 million capex.
  - Italy identified as more economical (20–30% cheaper labor for construction/operations); plan to optimize design (smaller footprint, process optimization) and target €15–20 million capex; engage an Italian engineering vendor for new estimates.
  - Capacity plan: prefer starting at 10 MT/year (dedicated initially to Nestlé), with flexibility to ramp 10–15 MT and optional “phase two” if demand warrants; avoid immediate 20 MT to limit capex/opex risk.
  - Conclusion: Germany paused due to cost; Italy pursued with optimized design and staged capacity (10 MT start, expand based on demand clarity).
- Capacity allocation and demand outlook
  - EU trypsin costs higher than China; demand growth stable; risk of overcapacity acknowledged.
  - Allocation concept: EU capacity for fast lead times and regulated/local needs; China for lower-cost, longer lead-time supply.
  - Nestlé demand references: Germany 18–20 MT/year; additional few MT in China and Brazil; some regions can use direct supply; Germany may need “work back.”
  - Lucas brand context: intends to list Chinese site as first supplier after virus validation and increase purchases; rumors of Argentina factory closure could shift volume to external suppliers, benefiting China capacity; EU site to cover regulated/local needs. Lucas is funding virus studies; China virus validation in progress for Lucas.
  - Conclusion: Plan for flexible allocation between China and EU based on regulatory/lead-time needs; near-term China demand likely supported by Lucas; EU demand to be clarified.
- Ownership and integration status of “Lucas/Mukha” brand within Nestlé ecosystem
  - No official internal or public confirmation found regarding sale; indirect discussions suggest possible sale; integration has lagged historically (7–9 years).
  - Some units operated independently and have been integrating gradually in the past three years.
  - Conclusion: Brand ownership and procurement integration remain unclear; internal confirmation needed.
- Commercial agreement duration, pricing, and volume structures
  - Movement from 1-year to 3-year agreement seen as positive; 3-year baseline preferred; 5-year possible with better pricing.
  - Pricing challenges persist; Nestlé expects strong justification, referencing similar current prices across suppliers; idea to reference “market fair price” rather than fixed numbers (e.g., commit >10 MT/year with market-based pricing).
  - Two supplier scenarios discussed:
    - Scenario A: 3-year contract, no operating profit in years 1–3; from year 4 pricing moves toward market as costs decrease.
    - Scenario B: 5-year contract with minimal operating profit in years 2–5; starting price higher than Scenario A for sustainability.
  - Buyer argues longer terms should be more competitive; internal approvals for 3–5 year commitments are difficult—shorter duration with higher volumes is easier.
  - Price thresholds and gap: supplier indicates pricing below “855” (currency unspecified) is currently infeasible due to CAPEX/OPEX and QC costs; buyer indicates moving “beyond 800” is very hard to accept; consider volume-linked scenarios to close the gap.
  - Multi-volume scenarios proposed: 5 MT/year (worst), 7–10 MT/year (medium), 10–15 MT/year (high). Supplier seeks a minimum of 5 MT/year (not a capacity cap), with better pricing at higher utilization and triggers for phase two expansion.
  - Consideration of a market-referenced pricing mechanism (e.g., indexation to “market fair price”) and avoiding explicit price commitments during stakeholder meetings.
  - Conclusion: No agreement on duration or pricing; directionally a 3-year term with volume ramps and a market-referenced pricing framework is under discussion; price gap to be addressed via volume/utilization improvements.
- Technology transition risk (animal-derived vs. microbial)
  - Major uncertainty around potential shift to microbial fermentation trypsin; timing unknown; procurement notes the decision is largely financial/business absent regulatory drivers.
  - Supplier concerned about being an interim bridge, risking stranded CAPEX; suggestion to position animal-based technology’s safety and reliability (referencing a recent microbial-related recall) in Switzerland/Finland briefings.
  - Long-term agreement with committed volumes and compensation clauses if Nestlé switches during term proposed as primary safeguard; supplier encouraged to propose such terms.
  - Conclusion: Technology-switch risk acknowledged; parties exploring contractual protections and narrative positioning but no legal guarantees yet.
- Meeting strategy, governance, and timeline
  - Switzerland meeting intended to align stakeholders, showcase capabilities, and influence internal authorization for factory engagement; avoid detailed price discussions in-session due to internal politics.
  - Target to submit a refined proposal in the coming weeks and aim for agreement signing by June; continue engineering work in parallel to enable rapid ramp-up post-signing.
  - Stakeholder coordination (e.g., “John”) to be managed; internal signatories may include Julian and senior leadership depending on volume.
  - Conclusion: Use upcoming meetings for alignment and momentum; progress commercial alignment ahead of HQ visit to avoid surprises.
- Regulatory constraints and sourcing history
  - Past halt in sourcing from China was due to Mexican regulatory issues related to China-origin materials; stakeholders understand supplier capabilities; regulatory pathways remain key barriers for certain regions.
  - EFSA and EU GMP validation activities planned to support EU market entry and dossier submissions.
